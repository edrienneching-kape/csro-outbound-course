Build Your Own Outbound AI Agents in Zendesk

CSRO learning course · Edrienne Ching · started 26 Nov 2026

How this course works

By the end you will have built four working outbound agents in a Zendesk sandbox, with Claude writing every line of code and you directing it. You do not need to read code. You need to know what each piece does, what to ask Claude for, and how to check its work.

The course has two halves that run together. This page is the syllabus and your workbook: each module's goal, checklist, exercise and KPIs, and the progress tracker at the bottom. The live lessons happen in chat, one module at a time: before each one I ask where you are starting from, teach only what you don't have yet, and hand you a hands-on exercise to run in the sandbox. The Next steps panel on the right is the single ordered list of what to do now.

Three rules that hold for the whole course:

  • You build one shared skeleton (Module 3), then plug each use case into it. Four use cases, one codebase, one approval gate.
  • Nothing sends to a real customer during the pilot. Every draft lands in Zendesk as an internal note or a pending ticket for a human (Mabel or Audrey) to approve.
  • Every module ends with you explaining back what you built and running a test in the sandbox. If you can't explain it, we haven't finished the module.

Time guide: Modules 0 to 3 are about two hours each. Each use-case module is about two hours of building plus a week of pilot observation. Module 8 is a half-day. Plan on about 8 weeks end to end if you do one module a week.

What we reuse from ARIA

ARIA Copilot (owners Gena Puro and Yvan Lin) already has a working brain, a knowledge base, a policy-in-code pattern and a QA loop. We reuse the knowledge, the rules and the patterns. We do not fork the app.

What ARIA is, in one paragraph: a private Zendesk sidebar app, one install per brand, that drafts the agent's next reply from the brand's knowledge (Help Center and wiki articles, macros, AI Response Rules, ongoing issues, streaming procedures, pricing). Two Claude calls per draft (Haiku reads the ticket, Sonnet writes), with deterministic code before, between and after that enforces refund, cancellation, streaming-compensation and money-back rules. About 104,000 drafts a month at roughly 5 cents each. Nothing is ever sent automatically. Behind it, ARIA Labs on an internal VM collects every draft and reaction and turns flags into fixes. Its predecessor, the Presales CoPilot, was retired; its brain document still holds the selling rules.

Why not run outbound inside ARIA

  • ARIA is a sidebar: it only runs when an agent opens a ticket and clicks. Outbound runs on a schedule with nobody looking.
  • ARIA is built to never change a ticket; its Zendesk access from the VM is read-only by team rule and any write needs CTO sign-off. Outbound creates tickets and posts replies. That sign-off is one we need anyway, for production, and it is better asked for a small new script than for a change to ARIA.
  • ARIA blocks XV PreSale tickets today (unmapped brand), and its core is bundled per brand app, so every change is a repackage and upload. Our yaml-per-use-case design exists to avoid exactly that.

1The mental model

Goal: you can draw an outbound agent on a whiteboard and name its seven pieces, and you can say which of the two ways of running it in Zendesk we use and why.

An outbound agent is not a chatbot. It is a small program that runs on a schedule, looks for people who meet a rule, writes them a message, and waits for a human to say yes. Claude is only one piece of it (the writer). The rest is plain plumbing that Claude Code will write for you. The seven pieces, in order:

  1. Trigger: what starts a run. A schedule (every morning at 9 SGT), a Zendesk trigger firing a webhook, or you pressing a button.
  2. Data: where the list of people comes from. A Zendesk search (non-converted presales tickets), a HubSpot export, a tag Alex Goldby's team adds, or a cleaned spreadsheet.
  3. Rules: who is in and who is out before any writing happens. Opted out, contacted in the last 30 days, already converted, wrong brand, no consent record: out.
  4. Brain: the Claude API call. It gets the customer's context plus an approved macro or template and returns a draft. It never invents prices, promos or claims; those come from the template.
  5. Approval gate: the draft lands in Zendesk as an internal note on a ticket tagged for review. A human approves, edits or rejects. During the pilot nothing passes this gate automatically.
  6. Send: after approval, the agent posts the reply as a public comment and the ticket goes out through Zendesk's normal email channel.
  7. Log: every run writes what it did (found, drafted, approved, sent, replied) to a sheet or a Zendesk view, so the KPIs are counted by the machine, not by hand.

How to direct Claude Code as a non-coder

  • Say what you want to happen and what must never happen ("never post a public comment in this script"). Constraints are more useful than instructions.
  • Ask it to explain before it runs, and to show you what it will change. If the explanation is confusing, say so; it will simplify.
  • Ask for a dry-run mode on everything: --dry-run prints what it would do without doing it.
  • When something breaks, paste the error back and say "fix this and tell me what caused it". Do not try to read the code.
  • Keep DECISIONS.md current with the rules you've set. Claude Code reads it every session.

Goal: one pipeline that every use case plugs into, so adding a brand or a use case means changing a config file, not writing a new agent.

Brand config starts with what you already use in production: XV brand 96314 and presales form 3819, CG brand 17272303923227 and form 17272095776411, PIA brand 17670847246875 and form 17756948008347. The sandbox has its own IDs; the file holds both and run.py picks by environment.

Guardrails in guardrails.py, not switchable per use case

  • No contact if the requester has the tag outbound_optout, or an unsubscribe in any prior ticket, or is on the suppression list.
  • No contact if any outbound ticket to this email was created in the last 30 days, across all use cases.
  • No contact if the customer converted since the list was built (re-check the Sales Opportunity field 42045988437275 at run time).
  • Every message carries the brand's opt-out line and comes from a real agent name.
  • No money-back-guarantee selling, no urgency language, no more than two touches per lead ever. The brain is told this, and guardrails.py checks the draft for banned phrases before it is queued.
  • During the pilot the approval gate is hard-coded on. Turning it off is a code change in Module 8, not a config switch.

The approval gate in Zendesk

  1. The agent creates the outbound ticket (or reopens the original one for use case 2) with the draft as an internal note, status Pending, tags outbound_ai, outbound_review, plus the use-case tag.
  2. A view "Outbound — awaiting review" shows them to Mabel and Audrey.
  3. They read, edit if needed, and apply the macro "Outbound → Approve and send", which copies the note to a public reply and swaps outbound_review for outbound_sent. Or "Outbound → Reject", which adds outbound_rejected and a reason.
  4. run.py --log counts the tags each morning and writes the numbers to the KPI sheet.

3bContinuing the conversation and escalating

Goal: when a customer replies, the agent answers within a defined scope and hands the ticket to Mabel or Audrey the moment the conversation needs a human. Part of the shared skeleton, so every use case gets it.

This is the same thing Presales AI already does inbound, pointed at outbound tickets. The agent that opens a conversation is pieces 1 to 7. The agent that continues it is an eighth piece, the reply handler, and it reuses the brain, the guardrails and the approval gate.

How a reply flows

  1. The customer replies on the outbound ticket. A Zendesk trigger ("requester comment on a ticket tagged outbound_ai") fires a webhook to the script, or the script polls every 10 minutes for tagged tickets in status Open. Polling is simpler and fine for the pilot.
  2. run.py --replies reads the whole thread, checks the guardrails (opt-out words in the reply suppress everything and tag outbound_optout), and asks the brain for one of two outcomes: a draft reply, or an escalation with a reason.
  3. A draft reply lands as an internal note with tag outbound_review, exactly like a first touch. In the pilot a human approves every turn. After Module 8's exit test, in-scope replies can go without review while escalations always reach a person.
  4. An escalation assigns the ticket to the outbound group, adds outbound_escalated and the reason tag, and writes a three-line internal note: what the customer wants, what the AI already said, what the human needs to do. Mabel or Audrey take it from there in their normal view.
  5. Every turn is logged: replies received, answered by AI, escalated (by reason), resolved by human, converted.
Always escalateAI may answer (pilot scope)

Account verification, login, billing historyHow the promo works and when it ends, from the template
Refund, cancellation, chargeback or MBG questionsDevice and platform compatibility, from approved answers

The brain's reply instruction has three parts the template forces: answer only from the approved answers and the thread, never invent a price or a promise, and if unsure say a teammate will pick this up and escalate. "Unsure" is a real output; the brain returns a confidence and anything under the threshold in the yaml escalates.

Exercise 3b.2 (20 minutes)

KPIs across all use cases: share of replies answered by AI without escalation (expect 40% in the pilot), escalation reasons by count (the top reason is what to add to the approved answers next), human time to pick up an escalation (under 4 working hours), and conversion on escalated conversations versus AI-only ones.

Risks: a reply handler that answers too eagerly is the one way this programme can produce a bad customer experience, so the escalate list starts long and shrinks only with evidence; a customer who replies from a different email creates a new ticket outside the tags, so match on email and merge; the approval gate on replies slows the conversation, so the reviewer view needs a "replies waiting" sort at the top during the pilot.

6Use case 3: Streaming fix outreach

Goal: when Alex Goldby's team confirms a reported streaming issue is fixed, every customer who reported it gets a short "it's working again" message, approved by a human. Service recovery first, revenue second.

The new piece is the trigger: not a schedule, not a list, but a confirmation from another team. Build it as a tag, because a tag is something Alex's team can set without learning anything new.

  1. Agree with Alex's team on one "issue key": a problem ticket, a tag like streaming_netflix_uk, or a Jira link.
  2. When the fix is confirmed, someone on that team adds streaming_fixed to the problem ticket. That is the whole hand-off.
  3. The agent runs hourly, finds problem tickets with streaming_fixed but not streaming_notified, collects every linked incident ticket, and drafts one message per customer.
  4. When all drafts are queued, it adds streaming_notified so it never runs twice for the same fix.
